How to start a lawn care business

SHORT ANSWER

Start with mowing, trimming and cleanups, which in many places need only a registered business, a local business license and insurance. Weed control and other pesticide work for hire usually needs a state license, so add it later. Buy reliable used equipment, price each lawn per visit based on your time on site and the drive, and pack your customers into a few neighborhoods so every day is a tight route.

By Matt, Dialbridge. Updated October 26, 2026.

Decide what you will sell first

Before you buy a mower, decide what kind of lawn care business this is. It changes your licenses, your insurance and your equipment.

Start hereMowing, trimming, edging, cleanups

In many places, a registered business, a local business license and insurance cover it. You can start this spring.

Add laterWeed control, insect treatments, fertilizing programs

Applying pesticides for hire usually needs a state license. Check your state's fertilizer rules too, and plan time to get licensed.

Weed killers are herbicides, and the EPA lists herbicides among pesticides. The EPA also says federal law requires certification for anyone applying restricted use pesticides, and that many states require certification for commercial use of all pesticides, not just restricted ones. The BLS says most states require licensing for workers who apply pesticides.

Here is how one state words it. The Texas Department of Agriculture says anyone who applies any pesticide, including general-use products, to lawns, trees, ornamental plants or weeds for hire must be licensed, and that applicators who apply only fertilizer do not need a TDA license. Your state will have its own version. Find your state's pesticide agency in the National Pesticide Information Center's list, a project of Oregon State University and the EPA.

Do not spray weed killer for a customer "just this once" until you have checked your state's rules. Mowing pays the bills in year one. Treatments can be your year two upsell.

On insurance: the SBA describes general liability as coverage for bodily injury and property damage. In lawn care that means a rock thrown through a window or a trimmer line that scratches a car. Tell your agent you will tow a trailer for work, because a personal auto policy may not cover business use. And the SBA lists workers' compensation as required for businesses with employees.

Play with the drive time. Going from 10 to 20 minutes between lawns raises the price you need on every lawn, with the same mower and the same customers. That is why the route matters as much as the equipment.

My stance: buy the best used mower you can afford and keep a cheap backup trimmer. A broken trimmer costs you a day of lawns. A brand-new zero-turn in month one mostly costs you sleep.

Leave the clippings on the lawn unless the customer pays for bagging. University of Wisconsin extension advises not collecting clippings unless they are needed or are thick enough to smother the turf. Bagging takes time, and dumping costs money: King County, Washington charges $129.38 a ton to drop off yard waste in 2026.

How to price lawn care

There are three common ways to charge, and most owners use all three:

  • Per visit for weekly or every-other-week mowing. Base it on the lawn's size and your minutes on site, like the calculator above.
  • Monthly or seasonal plans that bill the same amount each month. Customers like predictable bills, and you get steadier cash.
  • By the job for spring and fall cleanups, mulch and overgrown lawns, quoted after you see the yard.

Weekly versus every two weeks is where new owners lose money. University of Wisconsin extension says never to remove more than one-third of the leaf tissue in one mowing, that it is better to mow when needed than on a fixed schedule, and that lawns may need mowing twice a week in spring but only every two weeks in a hot, dry summer.

So price every-other-week visits higher than weekly, because the grass is longer and the cut takes more time. And write an overgrown-lawn fee into your terms, so a customer who skips three weeks in May pays for the extra work.

Build a tight weekly route

Ten lawns on three streets beat ten lawns spread across the county. Group customers by neighborhood and give each neighborhood a day. When a new customer calls, offer the day you are already on their street.

Two more route rules. Keep one half day a week open for rain makeups and new estimates. And once a street has two or three of your customers, knock on the neighbors' doors while you are there. They just watched you work.

Register the business

Follow the SBA's steps to launch a business. In short: pick a name, choose a structure, register with your state, get a free EIN from the IRS, apply for local licenses and open a business bank account. A sole proprietorship is simplest, but the SBA notes it does not separate your business and personal assets. An LLC protects owners from personal liability in most instances, and the SBA says registering usually costs under $300 in total. If you want help, the SBA's partners offer free or low-cost counseling in your area.

Getting your first customers

Lawns start growing in spring and that is when people start looking for help. Have your profile, prices and booking set up before the first warm weekend, not after.

  1. Start with your own street and the people you know. Five customers close together beat twenty scattered ones.
  2. Ask for a yard sign spot. A small sign on a happy customer's lawn for a week does what a mailer cannot.
  3. Knock on the neighbors' doors on the streets you already mow.
  4. Offer spring cleanups as a way in. A good cleanup can turn into a mowing customer.
  5. Ask for a Google review after the first few visits, once they have seen you show up on time.

Getting found on Google

Google says local results come down to relevance, distance and prominence, that more reviews and positive ratings can help, and that you cannot pay for a better local ranking. For a new lawn care company, that comes down to a few things:

  • A Google Business Profile. It is free. If customers do not visit you, Google says to remove your address and list the areas you serve, up to 20 of them. List the towns your route actually covers.
  • Reviews from real customers. Make your direct link with our free review link generator and text it after a visit. Never offer a free mow for a review. Google strictly prohibits incentives.
  • A simple website with online booking. Your services, the towns you cover, real photos, and a form that asks for the address and the service so you can quote without a phone call. See how to add a Book button to Google.
  • Fast replies in spring. Calls come in while the mower is running. A missed-call text back keeps those people from calling the next company.

Questions owners ask

Do I need a license to start a lawn care business?+

For mowing, trimming and cleanups, many places require only a registered business and a local business license, but requirements vary by state, county and city. Applying weed killer or other pesticides for hire usually needs a state license. Check with your state pesticide agency before you spray anything for a customer.

How much does it cost to start a lawn care business?+

It depends mostly on whether you buy new or used equipment and whether you already own a truck. The SBA says registering a business usually costs under $300. Use the calculator on this page with your own equipment, insurance and season length.

How do lawn care businesses charge?+

Most charge per visit for mowing, based on lawn size and time on site, offer monthly or seasonal plans, and quote cleanups and overgrown lawns by the job. Every-other-week mowing is usually priced higher than weekly because the grass is longer.

Do I need a license to fertilize lawns?+

It depends on your state. Texas, for example, says applicators who apply only fertilizer do not need a Texas Department of Agriculture license, but weed and feed products include a weed killer, and the EPA counts herbicides as pesticides. Ask your state pesticide agency.

What equipment do I need to start a lawn care business?+

A reliable mower, a string trimmer, an edger, a blower, a truck and a small trailer. Buy used if a mechanic checks it, and keep a backup trimmer. Wait on spreaders and sprayers until you are licensed for treatments.

When is the best time to start a lawn care business?+

Set up in winter so you are ready to take bookings in early spring, when lawns start growing. Register, insure, and have your Google profile and booking page ready before the first warm weekend.
