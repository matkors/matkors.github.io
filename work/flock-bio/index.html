They came to us with a list of keywords. That was the entire brief, and it turned out to be the right one.

01  ·  The problem

You cannot find these buyers by job title

Flock Bio sells pooled DNA libraries to scientists running CRISPR screens, protein and enzyme engineering, MPRAs and CAR-T work.

There is no job title that reliably identifies that person. Filtering LinkedIn by “Scientist” at biotech companies returns tens of thousands of people, almost none of whom are building a library this quarter.

What they had instead was a list of the phrases their buyers actually use. That turned out to be a better targeting signal than any firmographic filter.

02  ·  The constraint

One pass at a small list

The addressable audience is small enough that burning it has a real cost. There is no version of this where you send ten thousand messages and accept a low hit rate.

  • The buyers are technical. A generic message marks you instantly as someone who does not understand the work, which is disqualifying in a field where credibility is the product.
  • LinkedIn punishes volume. Connection limits and spam signals mean the channel itself constrains how much you can send.
  • Every message has to be worth receiving the first time, because there is no second list.
03  ·  What I built

Target the conversation, not the person

Rather than search for people who look right, the system finds posts about the right subject each week and harvests everyone who engaged with them.

Someone who comments on a post about directed evolution has self-selected far more precisely than any title filter could. Two n8n workflows do the work, plus a third that exists only to fail safely.

Workflow 1  ·  Sourcing and qualification

An Apify LinkedIn post scraper runs every three days against 19 quoted phrases — "gene library", "codon optimization", "directed evolution", "AAV cassette design", "gRNA libraries" and the rest — pulling the past week’s posts along with their comments and reactions.

Then two stages of filtering, mechanical before semantic.

  • Mechanical. Keep posts by people, drop company pages. Later, drop any profile URL containing /company/.
  • Semantic. An LLM reads each post and returns true or false on whether it is genuine industry discussion, rejecting personal stories and webinar pitches with a one-line reason.
  • Re-join. The model judges posts, but the leads are the engagers. A code step takes the approved post IDs and pulls back every comment and reaction attached to them.

Then deduplication by intent. The same person often reacts and comments on the same post. Each action is scored — post 3, comment 2, reaction 1 — keyed by person and post, and only their highest-intent action survives. A commenter is a warmer lead than a reactor, and the record should say so.

Everything lands in Clay, batched to stay inside rate limits, where waterfall enrichment fills in company, size and position. An LLM inside Clay then scores lead quality.

The scoring prompt was not written in a vacuum. We sent Flock Bio sample lists, they marked each lead good or not, and the criteria were rewritten against their answers until it matched what they actually wanted.

Workflow 2  ·  Personalised outreach through HeyReach

Approved leads flow from a Google Sheet into HeyReach every three days. An LLM first cleans the name, stripping titles, suffixes, emojis and pipes down to a clean first and last name, because “Dr. WALLIS MARGRAFF, Ph.D.” is not how you open a message.

A switch then routes each lead down one of two tracks, and this is where the personalisation actually lives:

  • Post authors get a message referencing their own post.
  • Engagers get a message referencing the post they engaged with, which needs the original author’s name in possessive form. A second LLM call formats it, so the opener reads “I saw you engaged with Jessica’s post” rather than something assembled by string concatenation.

Leads push into two separate HeyReach lists in batches of two with a fifteen second wait between them. The sheet is then stamped with the date, the workflow ID and the execution ID, so a lead is never contacted twice and every send is traceable to the run that made it.

Workflow 3  ·  Failing safely

An error workflow sits behind both of the others. When anything throws, it does not just alert — it deactivates the workflow first, then emails.

On a small list that ordering is the whole point. A bug that keeps running is a bug that burns prospects you cannot get back, so the system is built to stop itself before anyone reads the alert.

05  ·  What it taught me

Intent beats firmographics

The instinct in outbound is to describe the buyer and go find people who match the description. On a narrow technical list that fails, because the description does not distinguish the person building a library this quarter from the ten thousand who are not.

What someone posted about last week does distinguish them. Targeting the conversation rather than the person is the single decision that produced the reply rate.

The rest is discipline: enrich properly, dedupe by intent rather than by row, never contact anyone twice, and make the system stop itself when it breaks.
