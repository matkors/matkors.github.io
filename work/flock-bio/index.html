Flock Bio

RoleBuilt the system

They came to us with a list of keywords. That was the entire brief, and it turned out to be the right one.

01  ·  The problem

You cannot find these buyers by job title

There is no job title that reliably identifies a scientist about to build a DNA library.

Filtering LinkedIn by “Scientist” at biotech companies returns tens of thousands of people, almost none of whom are building one this quarter.

What Flock Bio had instead was a list of the phrases their buyers actually use. That turned out to be a better signal than any firmographic filter.

02  ·  The constraint

One pass at a small list

The addressable audience is small enough that burning it has a real cost. There is no version of this where you send 10,000 messages and accept a low hit rate.

  • The buyers are technical. A generic message marks you as someone who does not understand the work, which is disqualifying in a field where credibility is the product.
  • LinkedIn punishes volume. Connection limits and spam signals cap what you can send anyway.
03  ·  What I built

Target the conversation, not the person

Rather than search for people who look right, the system finds posts about the right subject each week and harvests everyone who engaged with them.

Someone who comments on a post about directed evolution has self-selected far more precisely than any title filter can manage. 2 n8n workflows do the work, plus a third that exists only to fail safely.

Workflow 1  ·  Sourcing and qualification

An Apify scraper runs every 3 days against 19 quoted phrases such as "gene library", "codon optimization", "directed evolution", "AAV cassette design", pulling the week’s posts along with their comments and reactions.

  • Mechanical filter. Keep posts by people, drop company pages.
  • Semantic filter. An LLM judges whether each post is genuine industry discussion, rejecting personal stories and webinar pitches.
  • Re-join. The model judges posts, but the leads are the engagers. A code step pulls back every comment and reaction attached to an approved post.
  • Dedupe by intent. Post 3, comment 2, reaction 1, keyed by person and post. A commenter is warmer than a reactor, so only their highest-intent action survives.

Everything lands in Clay, where waterfall enrichment fills in company, size and position, and an LLM scores lead quality.

The scoring prompt was not written in a vacuum. We sent Flock Bio sample lists, they marked each lead good or not, and the criteria were rewritten against their answers until it matched what they actually wanted.

Workflow 2  ·  Personalised outreach

Approved leads flow from a Google Sheet into HeyReach every 3 days. An LLM first strips titles, suffixes, emojis and pipes down to a clean first and last name, because “Dr. WALLIS MARGRAFF, Ph.D.” is not how you open a message.

  • Post authors get a message referencing their own post.
  • Engagers get one referencing the post they engaged with, which needs the original author’s name in possessive form. A second LLM call formats it, so the opener reads “I saw you engaged with Jessica’s post” rather than something assembled by string concatenation.

The sheet is then stamped with the date, the workflow ID and the execution ID, so nobody is contacted twice and every send traces back to the run that made it.

Workflow 3  ·  Failing safely

When anything throws, the error workflow deactivates the workflow first, then emails.

On a finite list that ordering is the point. A bug that keeps running burns prospects you cannot get back, so the system stops itself before anyone reads the alert.

05  ·  What it taught me

Intent beats firmographics

The instinct in outbound is to describe the buyer and go find people who match the description.

On a narrow technical list that fails, because the description does not separate the person building a library this quarter from the 10,000 who are not.

What someone posted about last week does separate them. Targeting the conversation rather than the person is the single decision that produced the reply rate.
