01

Callers cannot classify themselves

Health insurance is a category where people call confused.

  • They often cannot name the product they are calling about. They can only describe a situation: I lost my job, my daughter ages off my plan in March, my doctor is not on the list any more.
  • A phone menu asks that person to self-select into a category they cannot yet identify, so they pick wrong and either hang up or waste a licensed agent’s time.
  • The right answer is frequently a human. A system optimized for containment will fight that.

02

What the law requires before the agent is useful at all

  • Disclose that it is AI, early enough to matter and clearly enough that a distressed caller registers it.
  • State it is not the government. Nothing may imply endorsement by Medicare, CMS or any federal agency.
  • Carry the Medicare marketing disclaimer. Third-party marketing of Medicare Advantage carries CMS-mandated wording about not offering every plan in the caller’s area, plus the pointer to 1-800-MEDICARE.
  • Never assert an outcome it was not given. No eligibility, no premium, no coverage decision. Not a hedged version either.

That inverts the usual build. In a normal voice project the hard part is making the agent sound natural. Here the hard part is making it refuse cleanly, stay inside a defined scope, and hand off before it guesses.

03

How an agent decides what it may say

My framework, not theirs, so I can show it in full.

  • Answer. In scope, and the answer already exists in approved copy.
  • Answer with attached disclosure. In scope, but regulation attaches specific words. The wording fires every time, not when the model judges it relevant.
  • Refuse and redirect. Out of scope but adjacent. Say it cannot answer, say who can, offer the handoff.
  • Stop and transfer. Anything touching eligibility, price or a coverage decision goes straight to a licensed human.

Most teams ship an agent that treats the last rung as an error state. It is not an error, it is the product working. Designing it as a first-class path rather than a fallback is most of the job.

04

Testing something that is not allowed to be wrong

The refusal path is the product, so it gets tested hardest.

  • Write the adversarial cases first. “So will my insulin be covered?” in 6 phrasings, the polite ones and the desperate ones.
  • Treat every transfer as an assertion. A handoff that dials into nothing is worse than no agent at all, and it only shows up if you test the receiving end.
  • Regression-test disclosures after any prompt change. An edit that improves the greeting can stop required wording firing 3 turns later. That is a compliance event, not a bug.
  • Tune for silence. A correct answer that arrives 2 seconds late loses the call anyway.

05

Compliance is a design input, not a gate

I scoped all 3 from scratch: sat with non-technical executives, mapped the use cases, designed the flows, presented them back in plain language, and coordinated across product, engineering, compliance and operations. Nobody handed me a spec.

Every hour spent agreeing what the agent may not say saves a week of rework. Treating compliance as a design input at the start rather than a review at the end is the difference between an agent that reaches rollout and one that sits in review indefinitely.
