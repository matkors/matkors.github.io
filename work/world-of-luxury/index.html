World of Luxury

Luxury watches and jewelry, Aventura FL  ·  ~$500K/yr on Shopify  ·  worldofluxuryus.com ↗
RoleBuilt it and run it
Period8-month retainer
Catalogue~5,000 live SKUs
Match rate90–95%

The voice was never the hard part. The hard part was matching a misheard watch name against five thousand products that change every day, fast enough that the caller does not notice the pause.

01  ·  The problem

They had already tried a human

The showroom is open Monday to Friday, ten to five. Their customers are not.

People shop for a $40,000 watch at eleven at night and on Sunday afternoons. Those calls used to hit a voicemail box nobody was going to action before Monday.

They tried the obvious fix first and hired a virtual assistant. It did not work.

A VA who does not know the inventory cannot answer the only question that matters: do you have this piece, and what does it cost. Taking a message is not answering.

02  ·  The constraint

Five thousand products, changing daily

The agent had to hit live inventory mid-call, and fast. A lookup that takes eight seconds has already damaged the conversation.

The catalogue moves every day as pieces sell and new stock lands, so anything working from a static export is wrong within 24 hours.

Then the matching itself, which is where the real difficulty sat:

  • People mispronounce watch names. Jaeger-LeCoultre, Audemars Piguet, Breguet. Speech-to-text mangles them, and a mangled string matches nothing.
  • Model numbers resist matching. Digits, letters, dashes and symbols, inconsistent capitalisation, and a “pre-owned” marker that only some products carry.
  • Around a hundred products are near-duplicates, separated by price or two characters of reference. Exact match returns nothing. Loose match returns the wrong watch, confidently.
  • Callers ask about orders too, so the agent has to decide which kind of lookup it is running before it runs one.
03  ·  What I built

Six workflows in three layers

The agent is the visible part. Underneath it, six n8n workflows do the work.

Layer 1  ·  Keeping 5,000 products current

The obvious approach broke immediately. n8n’s Shopify node returns 50 records per call, and looping it hard enough to finish a full pull kept tripping limits and deactivating the workflow.

I replaced it with a custom HTTP request walking Shopify’s page_info cursor at roughly 250 records per iteration. Five times the throughput, and stable enough to run unattended.

It PATCHes into Airtable rather than creating, so a product that changed price updates in place instead of duplicating.

Layer 2  ·  Finding the right watch mid-call

Three lookup workflows sit behind webhooks the agent calls as tools. Each runs the same shape: extract the argument, normalise it, search Airtable, rank the candidates, format for speech.

  • 1Product nameLong and brand-heavy. This is what callers mispronounce and what speech-to-text mangles.
  • 2PriceThe tiebreaker. When a name hits a cluster of near-identical pieces, price separates them.
  • 3Model numberDigits, letters, dots, and a Preowned- prefix only some products carry.

It asks rather than guesses. When a model-number match returns more than one candidate, the agent requests the price or product name and narrows to one. Guessing confidently on a $40,000 watch is the worst available outcome.

A pronunciation dictionary fixes the input. None of the matching helps if the string leaving the agent is already wrong, so the agent holds the brand and model vocabulary and spells names correctly on the way into the workflow.

Layer 3  ·  Turning calls into something the owner uses

Every completed call writes the transcript, the recording and the caller into Airtable, including a link to the exact product they asked about.

A scheduled notifier assembles the overnight activity and emails it before the shop opens. The owner never opens a dashboard.

That report is what turned this from a phone system into something they renewed.

04  ·  The results

Coded by hand, not pulled from a dashboard

I went through every call in a recent sample and coded each one by arrival time, intent, outcome, and whether a lead was actually captured.

Nearly nine in ten calls land outside the hours anyone is there to answer them, and a large share are weekends.

72% of genuine inquiries become a captured lead. That figure excludes wrong numbers, people trying to reach a different dealer, one caller chasing a Macy’s order, and a couple who were abusive. Counting those would have flattered the number and taught me nothing.

The value range is what callers actually asked about: a Richard Mille at $302,500, a Jaeger-LeCoultre tourbillon at $41,000, a Corum at $21,000, plus Breguet, Patek Philippe and an Audemars Piguet Royal Oak Grande Complication.

05  ·  What it taught me

The failures are invisible in the success rate

This agent was not good at the start. It got good because I listen to real calls every week and fix what broke.

Three from the last pass, none of which show up in any metric:

  • A broken email domain. The agent was reading the contact address out as “world of luxurious dot com”. Every seller routed to email got an address that does not exist, and every one of those calls logged as successful.
  • Lead capture asked too late. Callers answered three or four product questions, then hung up the moment the agent asked for a name. The ask arrived cold at the end instead of being earned earlier.
  • No recovery from a misheard name. One caller’s name came through as “12”. The agent had no path back and the call timed out, losing a $21,000 inquiry at the final step.

A voice agent is not a build, it is an operation. The version that shipped in month one and the version running now share a prompt and almost nothing else.
